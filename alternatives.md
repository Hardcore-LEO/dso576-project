# Alternatives

This document explores how each stakeholder might decide today without our AI Workforce Investment Planner. It separates verified public evidence from plausible hypotheses and does not present an unverified internal process as fact.

## Candidate Vision A: California Workforce Development Board

**Role and decision**

The Executive Director of the California Workforce Development Board (CWDB) must decide which California regions and occupations should be prioritized for a new AI upskilling and workforce-transition pilot program.

### Current alternative without our tool

The likely alternative is a manual policy and grant-planning process using California EDD tables, regional plans, stakeholder input, staff analysis, and a grant-review scorecard. These sources do not provide one integrated view of labor demand, AI exposure, task change, job quality, and implementation readiness.

### Plausible decision process today

The following workflow is a hypothesis informed by public CWDB materials, not a verified description of an actual AI pilot process.

1. **Trigger:** A new funding opportunity, statewide workforce plan, legislative priority, or evidence of technology-driven job change might prompt CWDB to consider an AI workforce pilot.
2. **Information gathering:** Staff could review EDD employment projections, openings, wages, education requirements, regional plans, and program outcomes. Analysts might separately examine Anthropic, OpenAI, O*NET, and BLS data for changing occupations and tasks.
3. **Consultation:** CWDB could ask RPUs, Local Workforce Development Boards, employers, labor, workers, community organizations, and training providers to identify needs and test whether AI exposure represents a real local problem.
4. **Prioritization:** Staff might use a spreadsheet scorecard covering employment, projected change, wages, AI exposure, job quality, equity, employer participation, training capacity, cost, and feasibility.
5. **Pilot and approval:** Priorities could inform a request for applications. Reviewers might score eligible proposals before staff recommend projects or funding amounts to authorized decision-makers.
6. **Evaluation:** CWDB and its partners could monitor enrollment, completion, credential attainment, employment, earnings, job quality, employer participation, and worker experience before deciding whether to modify or expand the pilot.

### Information and people the process might rely on

**Information**

- California EDD occupational employment, projected openings, wages, education requirements, and SOC codes by available geography.
- Regional plans describing priority sectors, target populations, employer needs, and existing workforce partnerships.
- Existing program results, including participants served, training completion, credential attainment, employment, and earnings.
- Anthropic and OpenAI measures of AI exposure or use, connected to O*NET occupations, tasks, and work activities.
- Decision-critical information that the core data do not observe, including worker interest, employer commitments, local course capacity, program cost, accessibility, and effects on underserved groups.

**People**

- The CWDB Executive Director, Board members, and policy, research, and grant staff.
- California EDD Labor Market Information Division analysts.
- RPUs, Local Workforce Development Boards, and regional organizers.
- Employers, labor representatives, workers, and community-based organizations.
- Community colleges, apprenticeship programs, and other training providers.
- Independent application reviewers or subject-matter experts when a formal grant competition is used.

### Friction, uncertainty, and decision-critical gaps

- AI exposure is not the same as training need, automation suitability, or expected program impact.
- Anthropic and OpenAI measure different populations and types of AI activity; neither directly observes California workers or employers.
- The same occupation may involve different tasks across regions, industries, and employers.
- EDD labor-market geographies may require a crosswalk to CWDB's 15 RPUs.
- Public data do not show whether employers will participate, workers want the training, or appropriate training already exists locally.
- Equity, job quality, labor relations, privacy, safety, accessibility, and program cost could change the ranking.
- The Executive Director may set or recommend priorities, but the evidence does not establish that this role alone makes final grant awards.

### Evidence check

**Verified public evidence:** CWDB works through RPUs and partners across workforce boards, industry, labor, education, and communities. It publishes grant guidance and participant outcomes by RPU. A previous Regional Plan Implementation request for applications states that independent reviewers scored applications and rankings guided funding recommendations.

**Plausible but unverified:** Public sources do not describe an AI-specific process or confirm that CWDB combines AI-exposure and regional occupation data. The trigger, scorecard, consultation sequence, and Executive Director's authority remain hypotheses.

**Decision-critical gap:** A defensible recommendation would still require direct evidence about employer demand, worker needs, training availability, program cost, and likely distributional effects. Our tool could screen and compare candidates, but it could not replace those inputs.

## Candidate Vision B: Accenture

**Role and decision**

The Chief Leadership and Human Resources Officer at Accenture must decide which representative U.S. professional role groups to prioritize for the next round of advanced AI upskilling and workflow-redesign pilots.

### Current alternative without our tool

The likely alternative uses internal HR and skills systems, service-line demand, employee and manager input, leadership review, and small pilots. External data could provide a benchmark, but internal evidence is needed to translate U.S. occupations into Accenture roles and investments.

### Plausible decision process today

The following workflow is a hypothesis. Accenture's public reporting confirms its AI-skilling direction and scale, but not its internal role-selection or approval process.

1. **Trigger:** Annual workforce planning, new client demand, an expanded AI capability, a skills shortage, or results from an earlier learning program might prompt a new round of role-based pilots.
2. **Information gathering:** Talent and business teams could define role families and review headcount, skills, certifications, learning completion, project demand, utilization, hiring needs, AI adoption, and employee feedback.
3. **Consultation:** HR could work with service-line, project, learning, technology, employee, and risk representatives to identify important or difficult tasks suitable for AI assistance.
4. **Prioritization:** Leaders might compare business demand, skill gaps, affected employees, potential gains, cost, readiness, client relevance, and risk. Anthropic, OpenAI, O*NET, and BLS would be external context, not evidence of employee behavior.
5. **Pilot and approval:** A small number of roles and workflows could be selected for advanced training or workflow redesign. Each pilot might define approved tools, required human review, data restrictions, expected learning outcomes, and business metrics.
6. **Evaluation:** Accenture could compare skill gains, adoption, time saved, work quality, error rates, employee experience, utilization, and client outcomes before scaling, redesigning, or stopping a pilot.

### Information and people the process might rely on

**Information**

- Internal job architecture, role-level headcount, employee locations, service-line assignments, and task descriptions.
- Skills, certifications, learning completion, assessment results, and demand for new capabilities.
- Project pipeline, staffing gaps, utilization, work quality, productivity, and client demand.
- Employee and manager feedback about workflows, pain points, readiness, and concerns.
- Security, privacy, legal, responsible-AI, and client-contract constraints.
- External AI-use and exposure evidence mapped to O*NET occupations and tasks, plus BLS labor-market context.

**People**

- The Chief Leadership and Human Resources Officer and U.S. or global talent leaders.
- Learning and development, workforce-planning, recruiting, and people-analytics teams.
- Leaders from Accenture's major service groups.
- AI, data, enterprise-technology, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, finance, and responsible-AI teams.
- Project managers, employees in candidate role groups, and possibly client representatives.

### Friction, uncertainty, and decision-critical gaps

- Public sources do not provide Accenture headcount by role, skill gaps, project demand, training costs, productivity, or employee AI adoption.
- Accenture's internal roles may not map neatly to U.S. SOC occupations or O*NET tasks.
- OpenAI Signals describes aggregated consumer ChatGPT activity, not Accenture workforce behavior.
- Anthropic exposure and task penetration do not prove that a particular intervention will improve performance.
- U.S. occupation data cannot represent Accenture's full global workforce.
- Highly exposed roles may be poor pilot candidates when work involves confidential client data, regulated decisions, or high error costs.
- Public reporting does not reveal who approves role-level investments or how competing pilots are scored.

### Evidence check

**Verified public evidence:** Accenture reports that more than 550,000 employees completed generative-AI fundamentals by August 31, 2025 and that it had about 77,000 AI and Data professionals. It describes role-based boot camps and cross-service learning. Its FY2025 report records about $1 billion in learning investment and 47 million training hours.

**Plausible but unverified:** These sources make role prioritization plausible but do not disclose employee data, scoring criteria, governance, or approval steps. These remain hypotheses unless interviews or other documents verify them.

**Decision-critical gap:** A public-data prototype can create an externally informed shortlist of representative U.S. roles. It cannot determine Accenture's actual investment priorities without internal role counts, skills, demand, cost, risk, and pilot-performance data.

## Overall comparison and tool boundary

Vision A has a more publicly observable decision environment: regional structures, labor-market information, grant materials, and workforce outcomes are documented. Vision B is relevant and supported by Accenture's public commitment to AI skilling, but the actual decision depends more heavily on confidential company information.

For either Vision, our proposed tool could standardize external data, reveal why a region or role ranks highly, and produce an initial shortlist for review. It could not replace worker or employee input, local or internal operational data, risk review, stakeholder judgment, or the organization's formal funding and investment approval process.
